For the first commercial trials, use documented treated juice or purée and pasteurized/UHT dairy, make modest quantities, pack hygienically and maintain the cold chain. Add a validated in-house treatment only when the equipment and process have been qualified. The best method depends on the product, pack, sales rate, power reliability and desired life.

## Fruit juices: select the preservation route

| Route | Practical use | Conditions and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerated treated juice | Small daily batches; immediate-service drinks | Use treated inputs or a validated final process, hygienic filling and storage at 4°C or below. Refrigeration slows growth; it does not make untreated juice safe. |
| Pasteurization followed by chilling | A repeatable packaged juice line | Have a specialist establish treatment for the exact juice, pH, soluble solids, pulp, batch size and equipment. Record the coldest product temperature and hold, then cool and fill under the approved procedure. |
| Freezing | Seasonal fruit purchasing and controlled stock | Use a safe treated product, portion into freezer-compatible containers with expansion space, freeze promptly and hold at −18°C or colder. Thaw under refrigeration. Freezing is not a kill step. |
| Contract high-pressure processing | A potential later fresh-taste line | Assess an available processor, validated product/package compatibility, transport and cold chain. Do not assume ambient stability or local availability. |
| Validated hot-fill or aseptic processing | A later product designed for ambient distribution | Requires a product-specific scheduled process and compatible package/closure. A sealed bottle, hot filling alone or added lemon does not establish shelf stability. |

Untreated juice has caused illness. Wash sound whole fruit under potable running water before cutting, remove damaged areas and reject rotten fruit; cleaning is not a substitute for an effective treatment. Do not use soap on fruit. Keep raw fruit handling separate from treated juice and sanitized bottles. [N01]

There is no one safe time–temperature recipe for all pineapple, mango, citrus, avocado blends or hibiscus drinks. Acidity, solids and pulp change the process. FDA’s five-log pathogen-reduction framework is a useful technical benchmark from the United States, not a claim about Cameroon’s legal requirements. Have the actual product and process assessed. [N02]

For freezing, use containers intended for the freezer, allow appropriate expansion headspace and label each portion. Freezing preserves stock but does not sterilize it. Use potable ice only if it is a declared formula ingredient; adding ice or water after treatment can recontaminate the drink and changes the formula. [N03]

## A practical juice production sequence

1. Define one product: fruit variety, supplier, juice/purée proportion, pulp size, water, sugar and all other ingredients. Record fruit condition and lot. Keep pineapple juice, mango nectar and avocado blends as separate specifications.
2. Measure and record pH and °Brix with calibrated instruments. These characterize the batch; neither reading alone proves safety. Record the measurement temperature and instrument ID.
3. Use the established treatment for the whole product, or documented treated inputs under an approved hygienic assembly process. Do not borrow a dairy heating schedule for juice.
4. Clean and sanitize bottles, closures, tools and contact surfaces using their supplier instructions. Use packaging suited to the actual fill temperature and cooling method. Record closure/seal checks.
5. Chill through the qualified process, keep at 4°C or below, and transport in monitored insulated/refrigerated equipment. Put out small service quantities and replace them with fresh sealed stock; do not top up a partly used dispenser.
6. Assign the production lot and the storage instruction immediately. Set the use-by period only from a product-specific study that includes the intended distribution and foreseeable temperature variation.

Lemon, ginger, sugar, ascorbic acid and stabilizers may affect flavour, colour, acidity or texture. They do not independently validate preservation. Antioxidants that reduce browning do not make avocado or banana purée shelf-stable. Do not improvise preservative doses; any future additive system needs an identified food-grade ingredient, a permitted use, supplier specification and a validated formulation.

## Yogurt: preserve the fermented product

| Method | Best use | Key control |
|---|---|---|
| Controlled fermentation, rapid cooling and sealed chilled storage | Set, stirred and drinking yogurt | Follow H04, the actual culture instructions and a verified acidification curve; cool promptly and maintain 4°C or below. |
| Small individual cups or bottles | Retail and transport | Reduces repeated opening and portion handling; validate sealing, cleanliness and cooling of the filled pack. |
| Separate treated fruit preparation | Mango yogurt and fruit layers | Cool the fruit preparation hygienically, combine under a controlled process and reassess the final product’s pH and life. |
| Freezing | Frozen-yogurt bars, smoothies or baking ingredients | Freeze only sound, safely handled product. Expect texture separation on thawing; freezing does not rescue spoiled yogurt. |
| Heat treatment after fermentation | A different, professionally developed product | Changes live-culture status and texture. Ambient stability requires a validated process and package; it is not achieved by ordinary reheating and bottling. |

For the three yogurt recipes S-Y01–S-Y03, use the shared milk-treatment, culture and cooling plan in H04. The retained 6 g starter amount assumes a particular sachet mass. The current Yogourmet page specifies one sachet per litre and 42–44°C incubation; verify the actual sachet and regional instructions. Do not confuse the dry starter’s shelf life with finished yogurt’s life. [N05]

The source’s pH 4.5 cooling trigger is a development target. Record the time taken to reach it, not simply an overnight incubation time. A failed acidification curve must be investigated; adding more culture late or leaving the batch warm indefinitely is not an approved recovery. A fermented product still needs hygienic handling, cooling and storage. The heat-treatment and fermentation stages work together. [N06]

For set yogurt, ferment in the final suitable cups and minimize disturbance of the gel. For stirred yogurt, use a controlled stirring step after fermentation. For drinking yogurt, adjust the formulation deliberately rather than diluting finished yogurt with untreated water. Add treated fruit or flavouring with sanitized tools and record the lot and amount. Sugar and thickener improve formulation properties; they do not replace refrigeration.

Keep yogurt covered and use clean serving tools. Discard the entire container if mould develops. Freezing can change yogurt’s texture, making it more suitable for smoothies or baking than for a smooth spoonable product after thawing. Assess your actual product before choosing that route. [N07]

## Power loss, transport and service

Place independent thermometers or loggers in storage and transport. Keep doors closed during interruptions, record the start time and temperatures, and prioritize cold storage in the power plan. Do not judge safety by taste or smell. Isolate stock with unknown temperature history and obtain a disposition under the food-safety plan.

Use 4°C or below as the house refrigeration target. FDA consumer guidance uses a two-hour limit for perishables at room temperature, reduced to one hour above 32°C; this is an action threshold, not a validated commercial shelf life or a substitute for logged cold-chain control. Keep service quantities small and minimize exposure. [N09]

## Preservation trial and release sheet

| Record | Required observation |
|---|---|
| Product identity | Recipe version, fruit/milk/culture lots, ingredients and actual masses |
| Process specification | Equipment, batch size, treatment program or treated-input evidence |
| Measurements | pH, °Brix where relevant, temperature/hold readings and calibrated instrument IDs |
| Yogurt fermentation | Inoculation temperature, culture identity/dose, timed pH readings, endpoint |
| Cooling and filling | Start/end times, product temperatures, container type, seal result |
| Storage and delivery | Continuous or scheduled temperature records, outages, dispatch/receipt |
| Shelf-life study | Agreed sampling dates, microbiological assessment, sensory/physical changes, packaging integrity |
| Decision | Approved process and life, rejected lot or further trial, reviewer and date |

Start with three repeat process trials before a broader life study. Agree microbiological testing and acceptance criteria with a competent food laboratory for the product. Test the finished packed product, not just incoming milk or fruit. Include the expected transport conditions. No automatic 7-, 14-, 30- or 90-day selling period is assigned here. Sampling on days 0, 1, 3 and 7 is a study design option, not permission to sell through day 7.

Preservation should support flavour as well as safety: compare aroma, colour, separation, texture and customer acceptance while recording refrigeration, packaging and spoilage cost per sold portion. This is especially useful when deciding whether to sell chilled juice, frozen juice portions or yogurt bars from the same seasonal fruit supply.
