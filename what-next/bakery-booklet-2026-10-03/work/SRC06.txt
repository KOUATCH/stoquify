REFERENCE & PLANNING
Executive decision
Build the bakery around three revenue engines:
1.
Reliable daily bread volume.
2.
High-margin pastries, biscuits, cakes, and filled snacks.
3.
Lunch and afternoon traffic through shawarma, fries, soft serve, and popsicles.
The strongest defensible advantages are exact weights, dependable availability, clean production, attractive
presentation, reliable delivery, uniform B2B products, and local signature flavors. Do not launch all 24 source
products plus the additions below at once. Start with eight to ten products and expand only when quality, costs, and
repeat demand are proven.

CHAPTER 01
1. Corrections to the source documents
Rack yield
Some documents state 80 baguettes per rack and others state 101. If the rack has 16 trays and each tray holds
five baguettes, the yield is 80 baguettes. Do not promise 101 until the actual tray channels, dough length, spacing,
and molder output are measured.
Finished weight
A 300 g dough piece with 15% bake loss produces approximately 255 g, not 250 g. For a true 250 g finished loaf at
15% loss, scale approximately 294 g. Weigh ten raw pieces and ten cooled loaves to determine the real loss
percentage.

Water and ice
Ice is water. Record total water equivalent, not only liquid water. The 18 kg flour baguette formula with 10.38 kg
liquid water and 1.50 kg ice is approximately 66% total hydration.
Mixer capacity
The 50 kg rating may mean bowl capacity, dough capacity, or flour capacity. Confirm the manufacturer’s
specification. Begin with an 18 kg flour batch, monitor motor load and dough temperature, and increase only after
testing.
Oven cadence
The manuals combine a 25-minute oven cycle with 45-minute mixing intervals. The correct cadence is governed by
the slowest real step: proofing, rack loading, burner recovery, dough handling, or cooling. Time a complete rack
cycle on site before committing to a five-batch schedule.
Bread transport
Do not seal hot bread in airtight boxes. Condensation will soften the crust and increase mold risk. Use clean,
covered but ventilated crates until surface moisture is controlled. Use insulated cold boxes for dairy, meat, sauces,
and frozen products.
Combustion extraction
Diesel exhaust must never be directed toward customers or food. Use compliant extraction and keep combustion
fumes separate from any bakery aroma or customer area.
Egg coating
Do not brush raw egg yolk onto finished bread after baking. Use egg wash before baking or a validated food-safe
glaze after baking.

disciplines as the bakery pocket. The starting formula below produces approximately 100 large samosas at
120–125 g each, depending on filling moisture and fry loss.

For a meat version, replace up to 2.00 kg of potato with 2.00 kg of fully cooked minced beef or chicken. Keep the
meat filling hot enough during cooking, then cool it rapidly in shallow trays before assembly. Never use raw meat in
a filling that will only receive a short final fry.
Process
1.
Mix flour, salt, and seed if used. Rub in the oil, add water gradually, and knead to a firm, smooth dough. Rest
covered for 30 minutes.
2.
Cook the filling until dry and cohesive. Excess water causes bursting, soggy pastry, and short shelf life. Cool
to 4°C or below before assembly when the filling is not used immediately.
3.
Divide dough into approximately 40 g pieces. Roll each piece thin, or roll two pieces together with a light
oil-and-flour paste and separate after rolling. Cut into semicircles or use a standard template.

4.
Form a cone, add 40–45 g filling, and seal with flour-water paste. Remove trapped air and check the seam
by hand.
5.
Fry in filtered oil at 170–175°C until crisp and evenly golden, usually 3–5 minutes. For a baked option, brush
lightly with oil and bake at 200°C for 18–22 minutes.
6.
Drain on a rack rather than directly on absorbent paper. Cool before packing so condensation does not
soften the crust.
Commercial controls
Standardize dough piece weight, filling weight, seam width, oil temperature, fry time, drained weight, and breakage

refrigerate according to the local food-safety plan. Wheat is an allergen, and the meat version also requires a meat

5.8 Plain yeast donuts
The formula below produces approximately 100 ring donuts at 55–60 g dough weight after normal scaling and
handling loss. Use a cutter or depositor that keeps diameter, hole size, and thickness consistent.
Formula
Ingredient or component
Quantity and specification
Bread flour:
3.50 kg.
Milk or water:
1.30 kg, chilled as needed.
Sugar:
420 g.
Whole egg:
350 g.
Butter or bakery margarine:
350 g.
Instant yeast:
105 g.

52 g.

18 g.

Process
1.
Mix flour, liquid, egg, sugar, yeast, and flavoring until the dough begins to form. Add salt and fat after the
dough has developed some strength.
2.
Mix to a smooth, elastic dough with moderate gluten development. Target a final dough temperature of
24–26°C.
3.
Bulk-ferment covered for 45–60 minutes. Roll to 10–12 mm, cut, and place on lightly oiled screens. Avoid
excess flour because it darkens frying oil.
4.
Proof at approximately 28–30°C and 70–75% relative humidity until light and springy, generally 30–45
minutes. Do not overproof; weak donuts collapse during transfer.
5.
Fry in clean oil at 175–180°C, turning once, usually 90–120 seconds per side. Drain vertically or on a wire
rack and cool fully before coating.
Commercial controls
Record dough temperature, proof time, oil temperature, fry time, drained weight, oil age, and sale-by time. The
product should have a dry, fully cooked center, even color, and no raw flour band. Eggs, milk, wheat, and possible
soy in margarine must be declared and controlled.

Process
1.
Dissolve yeast and sugar in part of the warm liquid. Add flour, salt, spice, flavor, and the remaining liquid.
Mix to a smooth, soft batter with no dry pockets; it should drop slowly from a scoop.
2.
Cover and ferment at approximately 28–30°C for 45–75 minutes, depending on yeast strength and room
temperature. Stir down once if the batter becomes excessively gassy.
3.
Heat filtered oil to 170–175°C. Portion 40–45 g with a lightly oiled scoop, keeping pieces separated. Fry for
approximately 3–4 minutes, turning for even expansion and color.
4.
Drain on a rack. Serve plain, dusted with sugar, or pack only after surface oil has drained and the product
has cooled enough to avoid condensation.
Commercial controls
Control batter temperature, fermentation time, portion weight, oil recovery, internal cook, and drained yield. The
beignet should be light but not hollow with a wet center. Because the batter is a high-moisture product, validate the
hot-sale window and do not treat it as a long-life packaged snack.

The formulas are starting production specifications. Weights for prepared vegetables are after trimming; cooked

fine, shreddable crumb, a thin golden crust, and a clean butter aroma. Use it for breakfast loaves, braided loaves,
individual brioches, and premium buns. This is a general butter brioche formula; it is not a specification for the
regional Brioche Vendéenne.

on the bakery's flour, mixer, refrigerator, or rotary oven. Run the pilot, measure results, and approve a fixed
production version before scaling.

Preparation and temperature control
Prepare scales, a dough hook or spiral mixer, a calibrated probe, shallow food-grade trays with covers, a
refrigerator, suitable pans, and cooling racks. Grease or line pans before shaping. Keep the work area away from
the hot fryer and oven discharge.
Start with eggs and milk at approximately 2–5°C. Cut butter into small pieces and temper only until pliable,
approximately 16–18°C; it should bend without releasing oil. Target a finished dough temperature of 24–26°C. In a
hot room, use chilled flour if necessary and shorten handling time. Temperature and dough condition take
precedence over the suggested mixing minutes.
Mixing sequence
1.
Weigh every ingredient. Reserve the butter. Hold back approximately one fifth of the milk temporarily for
absorption adjustment; it remains part of the formula.
2.
Load flour, egg, most of the milk, yeast, sugar, salt, and vanilla. Do not leave concentrated salt resting on
yeast before mixing. Mix on low speed for approximately 4–5 minutes until no dry flour remains.
3.
Mix at the mixer's recommended development speed for approximately 6–10 minutes. Add the reserved milk
gradually if the dough can absorb it. Before butter addition, the dough should stretch into a thin membrane
rather than tear immediately.
4.
Return to low speed and add butter in four to six portions. Let each portion disperse before adding the next.
Scrape the bowl only with the mixer stopped. Budget approximately 8–12 minutes for incorporation, then
finish briefly at development speed if needed.
5.
Stop when the dough is smooth, elastic, satiny, and capable of a thin windowpane. A soft, slightly tacky
dough is normal. Oil streaks, a greasy bowl, or tearing indicate that the dough is not ready.
6.
Measure and record the final dough temperature. If the dough approaches 27°C before development is
complete, stop and cool it in shallow covered trays before continuing. Do not add unweighed flour to hide
stickiness or run the mixer until butter separates.
These times are trial ranges for machine setup, not a guaranteed total mixing duration. Approve the sequence
using dough temperature, gluten development, and the manufacturer's load limits.

First fermentation and overnight refrigeration
1.
Transfer to a lightly greased covered container. Allow approximately 45–60 minutes at 24–26°C, seeking
visible expansion without waiting for a full doubling.
2.
Fold gently, divide into shallow covered trays, and flatten to approximately 3–4 cm depth. Label with batch ID
and mixing time. Do not refrigerate the entire 11.45 kg mass in a deep tub.
3.
Refrigerate at a monitored 2–4°C for approximately 8–16 hours. Check the dough center during the first trial
to establish cooling performance. Gently degas once during early chilling if it is expanding strongly, then
keep covered.
4.
Shape when the dough is thoroughly chilled and firm enough to handle. Overnight refrigeration is a
production step, not a shelf-life guarantee. Record the actual cooling and storage temperatures under the
bakery's food-safety procedure.
Dividing and shaping
For individual brioches and buns, weigh 60 g or 80 g pieces, round with a taut but unbroken surface, and place
seam down in greased molds or on lined trays. For brioche à tête, reserve about one fifth of each piece for the
small head and seat it firmly in a deep indentation in the base.
For a 500 g loaf, divide into five 100 g balls and arrange in a greased open loaf pan. A pan near 20–22 × 10–11 ×
7–8 cm is a starting trial size; adjust the dough weight for its actual volume and desired crown. For a three-strand
braid, use two 167 g strands and one 166 g strand, braid loosely, and tuck the ends underneath.
Work in small batches so the remaining dough stays cold. If pieces shrink during rolling, rest them covered for
10–15 minutes in a cool area. If butter begins to smear, return the pieces to refrigeration. Avoid dusting with
enough flour to create dry seams.
Final proof and egg wash
Proof at approximately 26–28°C and 75–80% relative humidity, without condensation. Allow approximately 2–3
hours from cold dough, sometimes longer depending on yeast activity, piece size, and starting temperature. Keep
the proofer below 30°C for the initial trials to limit butter leakage.
Release for baking when pieces are visibly puffy, close to twice their shaped volume, and a light finger indentation
returns slowly. For the trial loaf pan, the dough should approach the rim with room for oven expansion. A clock
alone is not a proof test. A dent that springs back immediately suggests underproofing; collapse or a permanent
deep dent suggests overproofing.
For one 5 kg flour batch, prepare up to 500 g beaten whole egg plus 50 g milk as egg wash. This is a working
allowance, not expected consumption; weigh leftovers for costing. Strain, keep refrigerated between uses, and
apply one thin coat immediately before baking without pooling around pan edges. Never brush raw egg onto baked
brioche. Keep the wash and brush separate from finished-product equipment.
