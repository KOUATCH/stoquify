1. The decision: a bakery first, a cold line by
qualification
Build repeat business around correct weights, consistent taste, clean handling and dependable delivery. Do not launch
28 products together. Start with nine bakery products after the basic premises, ingredient-storage and operator checks:
baguette B01, Kumba-style loaf B02, sandwich loaf B04, burger buns B05, milk rolls B07, plain/glazed donuts P01,
puff-puff P02, coconut biscuits P04 and banana cake P05. These are a trial assortment, not a promise that nine
simultaneous daily production campaigns will be profitable. Alternate biscuit and cake days and make sandwich
loaves/buns against preorders initially.
The most promising next tests are professional brioche B06 for weekend preorders, pain omelette F02 for breakfast,
beef-filled bread F03 for portable lunch, and chicken shawarma F04. Test burgers F01 and mini pizzas F06 after
measuring pan and oven congestion. These are credible menu directions, not a verified top-seller ranking. The market
research supports product familiarity more strongly than it supports Nkambe demand, volumes or margins.
Frozen products and yogurt are a second business system inside the bakery. Refrigeration, potable water, validated
treatment and cleaning, qualified packaging, trained staff and dependable power are release gates. Merely owning a
soft-serve or popsicle machine does not satisfy them. Commission the soft-serve machine with an approved commercial
mix before developing the custom D01 mix. Develop I01/I02 fruit sticks and Y01 set yogurt only after their independent
controls pass. Scoopable D02 and coated I04 bars require additional freezing/hardening capability; they are not
near-term production promises.
No new equipment purchase, supplier contact, regulatory submission or commercial commitment has been made. No
factory, laboratory, consumer or shelf-life trial has been performed for this edition.
How to use this edition
Use the recipe identifier in weighing, labeling, costing and trial records. Recipe cards are original adaptations informed
by the numbered sources; those sources support specific principles, not every gram or setting. Read H01–H05 before
running any card. Numerical operating ranges marked as trials are starting settings that require your flour, machine,
product size and environment to be checked. A numerical target is not evidence it was achieved.
The workbook is the editable costing and scale model. Its selected-case control changes one calculation model between
Low, Base and High opportunity scenarios. Amber/blue entries are inputs. Missing prices and operating costs
intentionally produce “INPUT NEEDED,” not a fictitious zero. Use the matching component names in each recipe. The
supplied production multiplier is a planned demonstration batch, not permission to exceed any machine or refrigerator
limit.

What must be measured locally
Purchasing power, catchment size, customer footfall, vendor margins, school demand, competing loaf weights, road
condition, transport duration, power reliability, water potability, seasonal fruit availability and affordable price points are
unresolved. Nkambe is not modeled as a smaller Douala with the same climate or spending. Log production-room
temperature and humidity instead of importing coastal proofing assumptions. No tricycle, remote shop, 2–3 km route,
finishing oven, generator or bread slicer is assumed simply because an older document mentioned one.
Run a seven-trading-day baseline: record hourly visitors, transactions, units by SKU, average basket, stockouts, returns,
reasons for refusal and repeat purchases. Observe at least five relevant outlets and weigh bought comparison products
where lawful. Ask 20–30 target customers about portion, sweetness, preferred buying times and budget, then test actual
paid choices rather than stated enthusiasm. This is directional discovery, not a representative statistical survey.

4. Product selection and launch gates
The longlist contains 42 entries, including explicitly marked flavor-family/variant entries rather than claiming every flavor
is an innovation. Twenty-eight are developed into cards. Nine form the initial bakery assortment. Development selection
balances category coverage requested by the owner with feasibility; it is not a blind selection of the 28 highest scores.
Scoring is transparent professional judgment, not a measured sales model. Each dimension runs 1–5: demand
evidence/familiarity 25%; equipment readiness 25%; ingredient accessibility 15%; simplicity 15%; controllable
storage/waste 10%; differentiation 10%. Weighted score = sum(score × weight)/5, on a 0–100 scale. A score of 5 means
strongest relative fit, not certainty. No direct Nkambe transaction dataset was found, so demand scores are capped at 4.
An unqualified cold chain caps readiness at 1–2 even when refrigerators and freezers are already owned.
The workbook exposes every component score and editable weights. Adjust only when there is evidence or an explicit
management choice; document the reason. A newly high score does not automatically approve a recipe for sale.
Cold-chain, cooking, licensing and machinery gates remain separate.

5. Shared operator standards
H01 — Hygiene, meat, eggs and controlled cooling
Use approved suppliers, potable water, cleanable surfaces and separate raw/cooked tools. Reject damaged tins,
spoiled ingredients and unknown cold-chain lots. Store raw meat below and apart from ready-to-eat ingredients.
Allocate different utensils and personnel tasks for raw poultry, cooked foods and bakery packing. Check flour, milk,
eggs, fish, groundnut, sesame, mustard and sauce allergens; “may contain” statements do not replace cleaning or
accurate supplier records [S34].
FoodSafety.gov gives practical cooking benchmarks of 71°C for ground meat and egg dishes, and 74°C for poultry and
reheated leftovers [S22]. Probe the coldest representative center with a calibrated sanitized thermometer; record time,
temperature and corrective action. For this edition’s internal service rule, hold hot perishables at 60°C or above and
chilled ones at 4°C or below. These conservative operating targets are not presented as Cameroonian statutory limits.
For cooked perishables to be stored, use active shallow-pan cooling. FDA’s reference benchmark is 57°C to 21°C within
2 hours and to 5°C or below within 6 hours total [S23, sections 3-501.14/.15]. The bakery’s stricter final storage target is
4°C. Map the thickest, largest and slowest-cooling portions; an ordinary fridge filled with hot deep pots is not a validated
cooler. If a limit fails or history is missing, quarantine and withhold sale; do not improvise repeated reheating as a
rescue. Qualified local review must establish disposition.
At trial stage, sell hot assembled foods promptly or use a monitored hot/chilled process. Log cumulative time outside
refrigeration during portioning and proofing; schedule immediate cooking. Use an internal maximum 2-hour ambient
preparation allowance, reduced to 1 hour in rooms above 32°C, as a conservative action threshold—not a permission to
leave every food out that long. Any product-specific formal time-as-control scheme needs its own approval and written
procedure. If the filling cannot remain safe during proofing, reduce batches or defer it.
H02 — Dairy mix treatment and freezing
Use homogenized pasteurized/UHT milk and cream with certificates. Mixing powder into pasteurized milk can
reintroduce contamination, so ingredient status alone does not validate the final mix. Obtain the actual STAB 2000
technical sheet and label. The proposed low-fat mixes use 0.40–0.45%, while the richer mix uses approximately 0.27%,
within the supplier’s differentiated range [S14]. A changed stabilizer requires a new formulation; do not substitute starch,
xanthan or an unnamed premix at the same dose.
Premix stabilizer into sugar before dispersing, allow supplier-directed hydration, and use a qualified dairy treatment
system. Guelph’s batch reference is at least 69°C for 30 minutes throughout the mix [S09]. This edition does not
translate continuous HTST seconds into an unvalidated pot process. A trained process specialist must establish the cold
point, agitation, thermometer accuracy, actual hold, lid/condensate management and sanitation. Additional heating
needed for supplier hydration must be specified in that approved program. If the pot cannot meet it, use a qualified
pasteurizer or approved ready mix; do not sell experimental pot-treated dairy.
Cool promptly to 4°C or below using a mapped cooling method; do not leave a 10 kg mix on the bench. Age at 2–4°C
only within the approved schedule. Treat post-process additions as ready-to-eat hazards. A stick blender is a dispersion
tool, not a 50–200 bar homogenizer; trials must determine stability with the specified pre-homogenized dairy [S10].
Air increases ice-cream volume, not edible ingredient mass. Overrun = (mass of equal volume liquid mix ÷ mass of
equal volume ice cream − 1) × 100. For a measured density of 1.08 kg/L and 30% overrun, 1 kg mix corresponds to
about 1.20 L product before transfer losses; this is an illustrative calculation, not a machine result [S12]. Use actual
density and mass portions for costing. Hardening and storage are separate capacities [S13]; do not put warm production
into an overloaded holding freezer.

H03 — Fruit ice and infusion controls
Acidity, ginger, sugar and freezing do not sterilize fruit. Use ready-to-eat treated inputs or a qualified product-specific
process. FDA’s juice framework emphasizes pathogen reduction, not a universal kettle temperature for every fruit [S24].
Record pH, °Brix, fruit solids, maximum pulp size, treatment, cooling and sanitary filling. Supplier hydration guidance for
Super Neutrose is not itself a pathogen-kill validation [S15]. If the treatment specification is missing, the formula is a
laboratory/pilot development card only and cannot be commercially released.
Use a cleaned refractometer calibrated with water and temperature compensation. °Brix is a sucrose-equivalent
refractive reading: acids and other soluble materials affect it. It is not an exact total-sugar assay. Hold fruit-source
composition constant during the first trial. The old habit of writing “18–22°Brix” without calculating syrup/juice solids has
been removed.
H04 — Yogurt fermentation and cooling
Yogurt uses defined lactic cultures, controlled heat treatment, incubation and refrigeration. Tetra Pak describes a
90–95°C, 5-minute milk treatment and pH-triggered cooling; the pot must be qualified for the real fortified milk [S18]. The
trial starter is current Yogourmet Original, 3 g per litre milk, containing S. thermophilus, L. bulgaricus and L. acidophilus
[S19, S20]. This is an accessible named trial choice, not necessarily the least-cost commercial culture. Obtain supplier
confirmation for added sugar/milk solids and planned production; switch to a commercial DVS culture only with its own
dosing units and instructions.
Calibrate the pH meter with pH 4.00 and 7.00 buffers at the appropriate temperature each day. Record milk lot, culture
lot, dose, inoculation temperature, readings during acidification and cooling. Start cooling at pH 4.5 as the trial endpoint;
this alone does not validate safety. Establish an acceptable time-to-pH curve over repeated trials. If a batch misses the
validated curve, shows gas or odor, or fails to acidify, quarantine it; do not taste or extend incubation indefinitely.
For small-cup trial engineering, aim to cool from incubation temperature to 20°C or below within 1 hour and to 4°C or
below within 2 hours after endpoint. These are proposed stricter internal cooling targets to verify, not an industrial
guarantee from a domestic refrigerator. Avoid disturbing set yogurt during gel formation. Stirred and drinking products
need different controlled shear. No ambient shelf-stable yogurt is authorized by these recipes.
H05 — Formula, scaling and shelf-life conventions
All ingredient amounts are grams; eggs exclude shells and prepared vegetables exclude trim. Water is potable. Instant
Red/Gold mean the specified instant dry type, not fresh or active dry yeast. A manufacturer-authorized conversion and a
fresh trial are required when yeast type changes; no gram-for-gram interchange is allowed. Flour strength and
absorption must be checked by supplier data and actual development, not by a T-number alone.
Each component table states pilot mass, baker’s percent if flour is present (flour = 100), otherwise percent of that
component, and a production multiplier. Dough hydration estimates include milk/egg/butter water, using declared
development assumptions. For multicomponent products, filling percentages are component percentages, not dough
baker’s percentages. Frozen and yogurt cards also report whole-product mix analysis where meaningful.
Mass reconciliation is explicit: component input minus handling/unretained allowance minus process loss equals
retained output. Process loss applies to post-handling material. Divide by stated count; scale ingredients, losses and
count by the same multiplier. Fried oil absorption and shell coatings are separate positive ingredient components.
Oil-bath charge/replacement, pan grease, release aids and packaging are not silently included in edible net weight:
record and cost them as process consumables/packaging. No remainder is hidden; theoretical component outputs are
allocated equally across the count. Round individual weights only after choosing a production specification, and record
any remainder or short count.
Loss percentages are planning hypotheses, not measurements. For cooking with fat drain and vapor, the initial
combined process-loss estimate must be separated in trials by weighing drains and pre/post-cook batches. Evaporation
during covered dairy treatment is initially modeled as zero; weigh before and after and update if material. Do not add
untreated water after pasteurization to recover a target mass. Packaging mass is separate from edible net weight.

8. Costing and commercial controls
The workbook separates ingredient input cost, process consumables, packaging, paid direct labor, energy, delivery,
unsold finished units and allocated overhead. Process losses already reduce yield; do not also apply the same “waste”
percentage to ingredient mass. Unsold-product waste is a different downstream quantity. Ingredient costs include
material lost during manufacture because it was purchased and used.
Measure
Calculation and interpretation
Ingredient cost
Sum input kg × current usable-kg price, including absorbed oil and
charged coating.
Variable batch cost
Ingredients + packaging + direct labor + energy + process
consumables + variable delivery.
Saleable versus sold
Produced units may be technically acceptable but unsold. Model sold
units as the smaller of available good units and demand; do not count
unsold stock as revenue.
Unit variable cost
Variable batch cost ÷ modeled sold units; more conservative than
dividing only by theoretical yield.
Contribution
Revenue minus variable costs. This is not net profit.
Allocated result
Contribution minus allocated overhead; still not a full company
net-profit forecast. Taxes, financing, capital outlay and working capital
are not invented.
Gross margin
Depends on the agreed accounting definition of cost of goods sold; do
not relabel contribution as gross margin.
Break-even
Fixed period cost ÷ positive unit contribution, with matching period and
product mix. Negative contribution cannot yield a meaningful positive
break-even quantity.
Each costing row has editable batch multiplier, expected demand, selling price, packaging per produced unit, direct
labor minutes/rate, energy per batch, delivery per batch, other process costs and overhead. Record diesel liters per
campaign and allocate by occupied bake minutes or a tested energy method; do not charge the entire burner startup to
every recipe. Shared bun dough must be allocated once between bun sales and assembled foods.
Low opportunity scenario assumes demand at 60% of the entered reference, ingredient prices at 120%, energy cost at
130%, energy availability at 60%, and process reject rate 12%. Base uses 100%, 100%, 100%, 85%, and 5%. High
opportunity uses 130%, 95%, 95%, 98%, and 2%. These are editable stress-test choices, not forecasts or measured
outage statistics. Energy availability changes capacity; energy-cost multiplier changes cost per produced batch. They
are different effects. Reduce production plans when availability/demand is low instead of assuming every batch will sell.
After seven selling days, compare taste, repeat purchase, average waiting time, technical rejects, sell-through and
contribution. Provisional management gates: no unresolved critical safety failure; three repeatable production batches;
at least 80% same-window sell-through for a test SKU; positive contribution at entered complete costs; and a reason to
prefer it over the product it displaces. The 80% threshold is a trial management choice, not an industry fact. Review
products below it, not blindly discount unsafe or ageing stock.

9. Safety, regulation, packaging and disruption
ANOR publicly identifies bread NC 213:2013 and general food hygiene NC 30:2001; its older mandatory list also
includes yogurt/dairy standards [S25, S26]. MINSANTE’s indexed guidance describes the Autorisation de Mise en
Consommation under Law 2018/020 [S27, S28]. Obtain the current full texts, amendments, application requirements,
product registrations, inspection rules and label requirements directly through the relevant authority before sale. This
desk research does not certify legal compliance or replace local professional advice.
Create a bilingual English/French working label where suitable: product name, net content basis, ingredient list in
descending order, allergens, batch, production date, validated use-by/best-before instruction, storage conditions,
business identification and required authorization details. Have local requirements checked. Never make “probiotic,”
“healthy,” “organic,” “butter,” “local cocoa” or protected-origin claims unless supported. Current website dietary badges
do not override actual ingredient labels, particularly for chocolate coating.
Cleaning and batch records
Dry-remove flour and crumbs before wet cleaning where appropriate. Disassemble equipment only after isolation under
the manual. Clean with a suitable detergent, rinse as required, sanitize at the chemical supplier’s specified
concentration/contact time, air dry and inspect; never invent a universal sanitizer dose. Check sanitizer with its
appropriate test strips. Keep electrical equipment, fryer oil and hot surfaces protected. Record pre-op release,
changeovers, allergen controls, pest observations and scheduled maintenance.
Batch record fields: recipe ID/version; date/operator; ingredient lots and actual weights; input mass; final dough or mix
temperature; mixing and rest times; proof or incubation readings; cooking/treatment cold-point record; cooling/freezing
curve; raw/finished weights; drainage, vapor and handling loss; overrun/Brix/pH where relevant; saleable count;
packaging lot; disposition; dispatch/receiving temperature; cleaning verification; supervisor release. Keep receipts and
costs linked to the same batch, not only to a menu name.
Power loss and product disposition
Log outage start, equipment alarms, door openings and product—not just air—temperatures. Stop drawing soft serve if
the machine cannot keep mix safe; isolate affected lots and record the last verified safe reading. Keep cold-store doors
closed and move product only through a qualified backup/transfer arrangement. Do not reset an alarm and assume the
mix is safe. Unknown time-temperature history means quarantine and no sale until a qualified disposition decision;
melting/re-freezing is not an automatic rescue.
For yogurt, power interruption during incubation can invalidate the acidification curve; record the pH/temperature
timeline and withhold the lot if the approved process is missed. For dairy cooling, a delayed cool is not fixed simply by
freezing later. For frozen sticks/bars, investigate softening, refreeze crystals and package leakage as well as microbial
time-temperature exposure. Backup power must be sized for starting currents, refrigeration duration and safely
ventilated fuel equipment by a competent electrician/installer.
Dispatch
Use clean covered but ventilated crates for still-cooling plain bread and suitable sealed packs only after condensation is
controlled. Delicate pastries need shallow protected layers. Hot savory food requires validated hot delivery, chilled dairy
a verified refrigerated/insulated route with sufficient coolant, and frozen goods a validated frozen route. A short distance
is not a temperature guarantee. Run a 15/30/60-minute worst-case route test with product loggers and packaging
checks; accept routes only against product-specific controls. No remote shop or vehicle is assumed owned.
